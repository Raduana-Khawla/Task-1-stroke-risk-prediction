1.1. Current stratification tools for stroke risk.
Stroke represents the second leading cause of death worldwide, affecting approximately 15 million individuals per year [1]. Several risk
factor scores are currently available to clinicians to stratify stroke risk
and guiding clinical decision making. These include, the CardiovascularRisk-Score-3 (QRISK3), CHA2DS2-VASc and until recently, the ABCD2
score which predicts stroke risk following a transient ischaemic attack (TIA) [2–4]. The receiver-operator-characteristic-area-under-curve
(ROC-AUC) of these tools range from 0.67 to 0.76 and have proven
useful in the clinical setting, however 25–30 % of people who suffer
stroke have no known risk factors [5]. Indeed, there is a need for
improvement of these risk scores. CHA2DS2-VASc has been criticised for
its poor specificity [6], which is an important consideration since the use
of anticoagulants carries a significant risk of bleeding [7]. The QRISK3
score has been criticised for underestimating cardiovascular risk and the
ABCD2 score has demonstrated poor sensitivity and specificity, hence it
is no longer recommended in UK guidelines by National-Institute-ofHealthcare-Excellence (NICE) [8].
These limitations can be attributed to a number of factors. Firstly,
most of these models are not generalisable to the average population and
are instead targeted for use in those with a history of atrial fibrillation
(AF) or transient-ischaemic-attack (TIA) [6]. Secondly, their scoring
tools exclude several risk factors for stroke, such as smoking, obesity and
a family history of stroke. These variables were excluded due to their
relative lack of significant effect during construction of these instruments [9]. However, these variables remain clinically relevant and
collectively explain a significant proportion of stroke risk [10]. Also,
their reliance on established epidemiological risk factors may exclude
novel stroke risk factors which may be found by analysis of other data
sources, such as imaging and laboratory tests. Thirdly, dichotomization
of risk variables to present or absent (rather than a continuous spectrum)
and not including interactions between variables, sacrifices the predictive power of models [11]. Finally, these models aim to predict the risk

field that shows promise in the development of such models is artificialintelligence (AI).
1.2. Artificial-intelligence
AI involves the process of perceiving, processing, and inferring information by machines or computer programs [13]. In the last decade
we have begun to see the capabilities of AI in impacting and improving
patient care, both in the context of stroke and other disease areas. Examples include e-ASPECTS (Brainomix limited), which is able to successfully identify possible candidates for thrombolysis and/or
thrombectomy [14] by interpreting computerised-tomography (CT)
brain scans and “InnerEye (Microsoft)” which processes radiological
investigations, identifying likely cancers and creating radiotherapy
plans for patients [15].
Today, large and varied datasets of routinely collected medical data
are readily available, which act as an attractive substrate for understanding the unexplained factors and/or interaction of factors driving
stroke risk.
Within these large datasets exists a wealth of variables. These variables are able to influence a given outcome directly, or indirectly as a
result of complex interactions between one-another. In some cases, AI is
capable of analysing these complex interactions and gaining insight into
how variables within a dataset interact and affect the overall outcome
[16]. This enables identification of both major and minor risk factors for
stroke and an understanding of how these factors interact with each
other to influence overall risk.
AI may therefore be invaluable in building a comprehensive tool to
predict stroke risk and guide clinical decision making.
Of the various categories of AI that exist, machine learning (ML) is
the most commonly used in predictive modelling within the healthcare
setting. ML is a subcategory of AI in which algorithms learn to improve
their performance through exposure to data alone [16]. Supervised
learning (SL) is the branch of ML which is most relevant to predictive modelling. SL uses labelled datasets to train algorithms to accurately
classify data and predict outcomes. It is the technology with most
applicability to predictive modelling of stroke [16]. Several SL methods
exist. Table 1 lists the most commonly used SL methods for predicting
the risk of stroke from routine hospital data.
Because many SL models are capable of providing insight into variable importance, certain SL models are well suited to disease prediction
and prognosis, novel risk factor identification, drug discovery, medical
image analysis and disease diagnosis. They are hence often the models of
choice for predicting the risk of stroke, however they require accurately
labelled and organised datasets, and have been criticised for a lack of
generalisability within medical research [17]. In this paper we provide
an overview of the current research using supervised ML to predict
stroke risk and identify novel risk factors for stroke.
